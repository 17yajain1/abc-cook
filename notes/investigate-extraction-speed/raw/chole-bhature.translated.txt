Now, soak it in water with baking soda; add half a teaspoon or 1/4 teaspoon of baking soda to the water and soak for 6 hours. Doing this will enhance the flavor inside the chana. Soak it and keep it aside.

Now, let's measure one cup and half cup of semolina (sooji). We will add this semolina to a bowl. It will help with many things; it's not just one task. Add water to it and dissolve. This is one cup of water. In just 5 minutes, your semolina will be soaked and ready. While waiting, we will do the other preparations.

For the bhatura dough, I am using refined flour (maida). I have taken 2 cups—this means I take four times the amount of flour compared to semolina. I have put the flour in a plate or a platform and made a well in the center. Into this, I am adding 2 teaspoons of salt and 2 teaspoons of sugar; I have taken equal amounts of salt and sugar. Along with that, add half a teaspoon (1/2 tsp) of edible baking soda. That’s all, do not add baking powder or anything else.

Now, you can see the soaked semolina has become fully soft. Remove the extra water from the soaked semolina. I have squeezed the extra water out with my hands so the recipe proportion won't get ruined. Whatever excess water is there, strain it out and then add the semolina to the flour. Now, mix it well with the flour.

We won’t add much water while kneading; the semolina will have absorbed most of it. Next, we will add yogurt to the dough; any normal yogurt from home is fine. Add 1 or 2 tablespoons of yogurt to start kneading the dough. Keep full control while kneading because the semolina softens the dough and makes it moist. You need to knead a tight dough—the dough should be a bit firm.

Add flour to adjust if you see any dry flour on top. With the yogurt and flour mixed, your dough will form. It won't be completely soft or extremely stiff; it should be just firm enough. You can see the dough is ready now—you will see it comes together smoothly.

Now, start kneading the dough for at least 7 minutes; whenever I make it, I set a 7-minute timer and knead. This helps develop strength and elasticity. After kneading, you will see the dough is smooth and elastic.

Now, add oil—I've added one tablespoon of refined oil. If you add oil earlier, it can cause some difficulty in developing the elasticity. Massage the oil well into the dough for 3 to 4 minutes. You can see the dough is getting very smooth and stretching well because of the oil. The dough becomes nice and soft but not sticky.

After mixing oil thoroughly, you can see the dough is smooth and perfect in texture. This is the key texture for bhatura dough; it should be soft and smooth but firm enough—not too soft that they get very thin when fried and don't puff up.

Take a bowl and add one tablespoon of oil. Grease the bowl with oil nicely by rotating the dough inside it, so the dough is coated all over with oil. After greasing, cover the dough with a cloth and then place a plate over it. Let it rest and ferment for 6 hours. You can make it after just 30 minutes too, but keeping for 5 hours gives better results.

After grinding, transfer the paste into a small tea utensil and add 1.5 cups of water for the sourness.

For sourness in chole, we use Malabar tamarind or anardana peel which gives a black color and the sour flavor. I took 8 to 10 pieces of dried amla (Indian gooseberry), which is a better way to follow the method. You can also add dry amla as I have done.

Add 2 teaspoons of tea leaves to this pot and bring it to a boil on the stove.

Light the pressure cooker for making chole. After the cooker heats, add 2 tablespoons of oil. We will prepare the tempering (tadka). Add whole garam masala: big cardamom and cinnamon are a must. Also add half a teaspoon black pepper, 1 green cardamom, and 4 to 5 cloves.

When you add these to hot oil, the cloves will crackle. Once they crackle, add the onion and anardana paste. Stir it quickly.

To increase the water for cooking, add salt now. After adding salt, keep stirring rapidly. When the pressure cooker lid starts releasing steam (whistling), reduce the heat and let it cook.

Add half a teaspoon ginger garlic paste after releasing steam, and then close the lid again. Cook the onions well for one minute. Now the onions are perfectly cooked.

I have blended 2 tomatoes into a puree. Add this tomato puree and cook together with the onions on medium-high heat to remove rawness from the tomato.

Meanwhile, check the soaked chickpeas; drain the water used for soaking with baking soda—they will not be used now. Our entire chickpeas are soaked and ready.

Now, the pressure has released; open the cooker. After that, add the soaked chana (chickpeas) to the masala. Also add half a teaspoon baking soda (the same as before), which helps the chana soften quickly. Without soda, the chana won’t soften well.

Add 1 peeled potato; this thickens the gravy beautifully.

Now strain and add the boiled water from amla and tea leaves mixture to this pot.

Mix everything well. We will cook the chole in less water; they won’t burn or dry out.

Add about 1.5 to 2 cups of normal water to the pot and mix thoroughly.

Cover the pressure cooker and cook on high flame until you hear 5 to 6 whistles.

After 5 to 6 whistles, reduce flame to low and cook for 10 more minutes on simmer.

The dough has the perfect texture now. From this amount of dough, you can make 10 medium-sized bhaturas.

Smooth the dough perfectly with no cracks or tears.

There are two ways to shape the bhaturas: One is simple rolling as you do for roti; the other is twisting the dough in hand like when making gulab jamun. You can do either.

Keep the rolled dough aside for a few minutes.

Now the dough seems a bit tight or tense; after making bhaturas, tension develops, causing them to shrink and reduce in size. To prevent this, let the tension relax.

The solution is simple: add a small royal touch (apply some oil or moisture lightly) to all dough balls and cover them with a cloth for at least 10 minutes. You can keep longer too if you want.

Take one dough ball, do not dust it with dry flour; that will spoil the oil and affect the brightness of the bhatura.

Touch the dough ball with the hot oil temperature and roll it out gently like you do for puris; ensure no cracks or tears. Roll it like a perfect circle; it doesn’t have to be very thin.

Heat enough oil on high flame. If the oil temperature is low, bhaturas become oily and do not puff well because the pressure isn’t enough.

Put the bhatura gently in hot oil.

As soon as it starts puffing, flip it over—that's the main secret. Flip quickly to create pressure on the other side and it will puff fully.

Fry the bhatura till golden brown on both sides.
